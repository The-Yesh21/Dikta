The transcription gap

Why standard speech readers fail on lined paper

Audiobooks and text-to-speech synthesizers are engineered for passive listening at 150 to 220 words per minute. Human handwriting averages 20 to 35 words per minute. Attempting to copy lecture notes from a continuous reader turns study into frustration: you write three words, scramble for your phone to pause, rewind five seconds, and lose your train of thought.

Continuous speech synthesizers

Built for passive audiobooks
  • ✕ Speaks continuously at ~160 WPM without pause.
  • ✕ Forces 40+ manual screen taps per notebook page to pause and rewind.
  • ✕ Ignores handwriting fatigue, causing messy notes and wrist strain.
  • ✕ Glides over complex technical words too fast to spell or transcribe.

Dikta handwriting pacing

Built for the stroke of a pen
  • ✓ Breaks sentences into 3 to 8 word grammatical clauses.
  • ✓ Calculates writing pauses based on your calibrated letter-per-minute speed.
  • ✓ Gently slows tempo as fatigue accumulates during long study sessions.
  • ✓ Spells technical terminology syllable by syllable just like a teacher.
The physics of handwriting

Calibrated to the stroke of a pen

Dikta does not use arbitrary delays. Every pause is dynamically calculated in pure Dart from character grapheme counts, punctuation weight, session duration, and real-time voice feedback.

The Dikta Pacing Equation
gap = ((n_graphemes / writing_speed) / tempo(t)) * syntax_factor * jitter - speech_duration
01

Stroke-level character timing

A word's length is not measured in raw bytes. Latin characters take ~0.28 seconds to write. Devanagari and Kannada conjuncts take 0.38s to 0.45s. The engine adjusts the writing window for the exact script family.

02

12-minute natural energy decay

Students start sessions with brisk energy and tire over time. Dikta's tempo decays along an exponential curve from 1.0 down to 0.70 (tau = 12 minutes), matching your natural writing stamina.

03

Restorative breath intervals

Fatigue is never a dead-end drop. Every 5 minutes or at major paragraph breaks, Dikta recovers 50% of the tempo drop, giving your hand a 2.5-second breath pause before beginning the next section.

04

Responsive to your voice

Saying "repeat" or "slower" immediately lowers your baseline writing speed by 0.03 words/second. Uninterrupted stretches let cadence rise smoothly.

Pedagogical dictation

How teachers dictate unfamiliar words

When an instructor introduces a technical term, they do not merely speak it once at normal speed. They pronounce the word, break it down syllable by syllable with deliberate letter spacing, and then reassemble it.
